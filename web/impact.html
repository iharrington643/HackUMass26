// impact areas

Who it helps

Making the treatment plan make sense

"Why every three weeks?" "Why did it stop working?" Those questions are hard to answer in a 15-minute appointment. OncoSphere shows the answer: drug levels rising and falling, sensitive cells dying, a resistant clone quietly taking over.

Why schedules matter

See why gaps between doses exist and what happens in them.

Why resistance happens

Not a failure of the patient or the drug, but evolution.

What to expect

The wellness sidebar times likely side effects from drug labels, like carboplatin's white-cell low point around day 21.

Better questions

Helps families come to appointments with clearer questions for their care team.

An educational tool, never a substitute for a doctor's advice about a specific person.

A pharmacology lab with no lab

Diffusion limits, dose-response curves, the oxygen effect in radiotherapy, clonal selection: topics usually taught with static diagrams. Here students change one number and watch the consequences unfold in 3D.

Live parameters

Every interactive plot on the science page doubles as a classroom demo.

Honest uncertainty

Teaches that predictions come with ranges, and how to check if a range is honest.

Data literacy

Labels on every number show the difference between sourced, fitted and guessed values.

Open code

Students can read, test and extend every model, from the solver to the ML.

Rethinking "hit it as hard as you can"

Our sensitivity analysis found that making chemo 50% stronger made resistance take over 74–85 days into treatment, in a scenario where it otherwise never did by day 180. Killing every sensitive cell removes the competition that held resistant cells back.

Competitive release

A real evolutionary effect behind adaptive therapy, which has been trialed in prostate cancer (Zhang et al., 2017).

Test schedules in silico

Compare dose, timing and combinations across virtual patients before anyone takes a pill.

Find the levers

Sensitivity analysis shows which biological numbers actually decide the outcome.

Virtual patients before real ones

Thousands of simulated patients with different mutation rates, growth speeds and drug sensitivities, run through every regimen. The surrogate model then gives answers in milliseconds, with calibrated ranges.

Screen fast

Simulate offline, learn, then predict instantly: the pattern needed to scale up to real models.

See the spread

Strategy charts show the range across patients, not just the average.

Grounded in data

Drug potency from ~1,000 GDSC cell lines and starting mutations from ~5,300 TCGA tumors.

No server, no license, no lab

The whole simulation and the ML model run inside a web browser. The kiosk version runs on a Raspberry Pi with no internet at all. The data sources are public.

Runs anywhere

A school laptop, a library computer, a phone-tethered Pi at a rural health fair.

Free and open

Open code and public data (GDSC, cBioPortal, drug labels) mean anyone can audit or adapt it.

Small footprint

A 2 MB single file version works by double-clicking, no install.

A tumor you can touch

Placing a color-coded pill bottle on the RFID reader picks the treatment; dials set dose and scrub through time. Each bottle's color is the resistance color on screen, so if the red bottle stops working you see red cells take over. No mouse, no screen literacy needed. (We also built a five-panel LED wall, but retired it for safety: here's why.)

Kids & museums

Science fairs and museum exhibits, where people learn by doing.

Accessibility

Large physical controls and color-matched bottles, plus full keyboard control on screen.

Memorable

Watching your bottle's color slowly take over the tumor sticks with you.

// scenarios

Imagine it in use

Illustrative scenarios of how OncoSphere could be used. These are examples, not real people.

A pharmacology lecture

The professor drags paclitaxel's penetration length from 2 to 40 cells. The class watches the starved core finally die, and understands why drug delivery is a research field of its own.

A family conversation

A teenager whose parent is starting chemo plays with the console at a hospital education center and finally gets why the cycles have breaks.

A research question

A grad student swaps in measured kill rates for one drug, reruns the sensitivity analysis overnight, and finds the dose window where resistance is delayed longest.

A science fair booth

Kids take turns choosing pill bottles and racing to keep the tumor blue before their bottle's color takes over. Resistance wins sometimes, and that's the lesson.

// responsibility

Using it responsibly

Not medical advice

OncoSphere predicts a simulation, not a person. It should never inform decisions about anyone's treatment.

Transparent by design

Every number carries a label (sourced, fitted, estimated or placeholder), and every source is listed.

Sensitive topic

Cancer touches many families. Side-effect info is framed gently and always points people back to their care team.
