// 01 · the tumor

A world on a grid

The tumor lives on a 3D grid inside a sphere. Each spot is the size of one cell. There are no blood vessels inside it: oxygen and drugs soak in from the edge, like a lab spheroid or a very young tumor.

// 02 · oxygen & drugs

Reaction-diffusion

Oxygen spreads in from the edge while cells burn it. The balance between spreading and burning is one equation, and its answer has a natural length scale: the penetration length L. Every drug follows the same rule with its own L.

0 = D∇²C − k·C   ⟹   L = √(D / k)// spreading in = being used up

How deep does it reach?

Steady-state level vs depth into a sphere of radius 20 cells. Oxygen has L ≈ 3 cells; drag to compare a drug.

Penetration length per drug

How far each drug gets before it falls to ~37%. Longer = reaches the starved core.
// 03 · evolution

Mutation, selection, resistance

Every division has a small chance to switch on a trait. Resistance always costs something (12% slower division per trait), so without treatment resistant cells lose. Under treatment they win. That trade-off is the whole story of drug resistance.

PRO

Faster division

Like KRAS, BRAF or PIK3CA driver mutations.

HYP

Survives low oxygen

Like TP53 loss: keeps dividing where others go dormant.

MUT

Mutator

Mutates 10× faster, like POLE or mismatch-repair defects.

RCH

Chemo resistance

Raises the dose a cell needs (the resistance fold).

EFX

Drug pump

Pumps drug out, cutting the inside level 6×.

RAD

Better DNA repair

Shrugs off radiation damage.

RTA

Target escape

Hormone or HER2 target stops mattering.

IMM

Immune escape

Hides from T cells, beats checkpoint inhibitors.

Competitive release, in two equations

Sensitive vs resistant cells sharing space, chemo every 21 days. A stronger drug clears the competition, so resistance takes over sooner.
drug strength: 1.0×   resistance cost: 12%
why it matters

The adaptive-therapy insight

Hitting a tumor as hard as possible wipes out the sensitive cells that were keeping the resistant ones in check. Our full 3D simulation found the same thing: making chemo 50% stronger made resistance take over 74–85 days into treatment in a scenario where it otherwise never did.

That's the reasoning behind adaptive therapy, which doses to control a tumor rather than eradicate it. It has been tested in prostate cancer patients (Zhang et al., 2017).

// pill bottles

One bottle, one color, one treatment

The console has eight color-coded RFID pill bottles. On screen, a cell that resists a treatment glows in that bottle's color. Put the red bottle on the reader, and if the tumor slowly fills with red, those are doxorubicin-resistant cells taking over. A cell that resists several treatments shows the color of the one you're using right now.

key 1 · red bottle

Doxorubicin

Resisted by: RCH or the drug pump (EFX)

Why red: doxorubicin really is red, nicknamed the "red devil".

key 2 · black bottle

Photofrin (porfimer)

Resisted by: RCH

Why black: patients have to stay out of bright light afterward.

key 3 · orange bottle

Goserelin

Resisted by: target escape (RTA)

Why orange: hormone therapy.

key 4 · pink bottle

Enhertu

Resisted by: RTA or the drug pump (EFX)

Why pink: it's for HER2 breast cancer (pink ribbon).

key 5 · yellow bottle

Radiation

Resisted by: better DNA repair (RAD)

Why yellow: the radiation warning sign.

key 6 · green bottle

Pembrolizumab

Resisted by: immune escape (IMM)

Why green: a green light for T cells.

key 7 · grey bottle

Carboplatin

Resisted by: RCH

Why grey: platinum is a grey metal.

key 8 · white bottle

Paclitaxel

Resisted by: RCH or the drug pump (EFX)

Why white: it comes from the pale bark of the yew tree.

Cells that aren't resistant use colors that are never bottle colors: dividing   resting (low oxygen)   persister   radiation-doomed   dead.

// 04 · treatments

How each treatment kills

Dose-response (Hill curve)

Kill rate vs local drug level. Resistant cells need many times more drug; GDSC cell-line data puts that fold at 8–19× for our chemo drugs.
resistance fold: 13.7×   Hill exponent: 2.0

Radiation: the linear-quadratic model

Fraction of cells surviving one dose. Cells without oxygen are about 3× harder to kill, which is why the starved core survives.
α: 0.30   α/β: 10 Gy

Chemo + persisters

Carboplatin, doxorubicin and paclitaxel kill on a Hill curve, weaker on resting cells. Under drug pressure, a few cells switch into a reversible drug-tolerant 'persister' state without any mutation (Sharma 2010).

Enhertu (antibody-drug conjugate)

The antibody barely gets past a few cell layers, but its released payload spreads further (the bystander effect). Each cell feels whichever is stronger.

Pembrolizumab

Releases the brakes on T cells, which diffuse in from the edge. Tumors with more mutations are easier to see (Samstein 2019). Immune-escape clones are invisible.

Photofrin + laser

The drug does nothing until the laser switches on, 2 days after injection, and it only kills where there's oxygen.

Goserelin

Shuts off hormone signaling, so it only works on hormone-driven cancers like prostate and some breast tumors. Target-escape cells shrug it off.

Radiation

Fractionated doses (e.g. 2 Gy × 30). Doomed cells die over the next ~30 hours, not instantly, just like real mitotic catastrophe.

// 05 · honesty

Every number is labeled

Some numbers come from papers, some from our own fits, and some are still placeholders. The code labels every one, so you always know which is which.

SOURCEDRAYNE

Straight from a paper, a drug label, or Rayne's research sheet. Examples: penetration depths, half-lives, side-effect rates, cancer statistics.

FITPROXY

Fitted to a measurement, or borrowed from a close relative. Examples: GDSC resistance folds (cisplatin for carboplatin), consumption rates fitted to measured depths.

ESTIMATEDUMMY

Our best guess from a range, or a placeholder. Example: most drug kill rates. The sensitivity analysis tells us which of these matter most.
